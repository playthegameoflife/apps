Why Your AI Prompts Feel Clear to You — But Keep Failing

Published September 30, 2026  |  8 min read  |  AI Prompting

You know what you mean. You typed it out carefully. You used complete sentences. You thought about what you wanted. And the AI gave you something… vanilla. Generic. Not what you had in mind at all.

Sound familiar? You're not alone — and it's not a problem with the AI. It's a problem with how we communicate with it.

Here's the uncomfortable truth most prompting guides don't tell you: the gap between "clear to you" and "clear to the AI" is enormous. And until you see it, you'll keep getting the same generic outputs no matter how carefully you craft your words.

The Illusion of Clarity in AI Prompting

When you write a prompt, your brain fills in enormous gaps automatically. You know who you're talking to. You know what you want. You know what would make the result actually useful. And because you know all that, it feels like you've communicated it.

You haven't.

AI doesn't have access to your mental model. It only has the words you typed. If those words don't explicitly encode your intent, the AI has to guess — and it guesses generic every time.

This is what one Reddit user described as: "No matter how I phrase it, I get the same response. The answers are so vanilla." Another put it more bluntly: "I have to push back to get a good answer."

The frustrating part? You genuinely thought you were being specific. That's what makes this bug so insidious — it feels like an AI problem, but it's a communication problem. And it's one you can fix.

Why "I'm Being Specific" Often Isn't

Let's make this concrete. Here are two prompts that feel similar to the person writing them:

Vague Prompt

"Write me an email about our new product feature."

Specific Prompt

"Write a re-engagement email to trial users who haven't logged in for 7+ days. The trial ends Friday. Goal: get them to book a 20-min onboarding call. Tone: friendly, not pushy. Under 150 words."

The vague prompt feels complete to you. It names the task clearly enough, right? The problem is everything it doesn't say:

  • Who is this email from?
  • Who is it going to?
  • What should the reader do after reading it?
  • What's the deadline or urgency?
  • What tone is appropriate?
  • How long should it be?

The AI, working from the vague prompt, has to answer all of those questions itself. And when AI fills in the blanks, it defaults to safe, generic, middle-of-the-road outputs — because it doesn't know any better.

The Three Missing Pieces That Make Prompts Vague

After analyzing hundreds of failed prompts (and seeing the same patterns repeat across Reddit threads and user feedback), three things are almost always missing when a prompt produces generic, unhelpful outputs:

1. The Goal (Not Just the Task)

Most prompts describe the task ("write an email") but not the goal ("get a trial user to book an onboarding call before Friday"). These are fundamentally different requests — even if they seem like the same thing to you.

The AI can't see the outcome you're trying to achieve. It only sees the action you've asked for. So when the goal is missing, it does the bare minimum of the task.

2. The Audience (With Specifics)

"Write something for my customers" is vague. "Write something for CTOs at Series B fintech companies who are skeptical of vendor claims" is specific. The audience details dramatically change the tone, complexity, assumptions, and call to action.

As one community member described their frustration: "I gave it all the context but it still missed the point." Usually, the "context" they gave was surface-level ("my customers") rather than the actual audience details that shape how a piece should be written.

3. The Constraints (What to Avoid)

Here's a pattern that shows up constantly in prompting frustrations: users say "no lists" but the AI gives a numbered list. Users say "don't be salesy" but get a pitch. The issue isn't that the AI is ignoring instructions — it's that constraints weren't explicit enough.

AI works best when you tell it what to do AND what not to do. Generic outputs are often the result of AI defaulting to the safest, most standard format (which usually means lists, bullet points, and middle-of-the-road language).

A Framework to Close the Gap: The GAC Method

The next time you're about to type a prompt into ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini, run it through this quick mental checklist before hitting send:

  • G — Goal: What does a successful output look like? What action should the reader take, or what should they know?
  • A — Audience: Who is reading this? What's their level of knowledge? What do they already believe about this topic?
  • C — Constraints: Format (bullets vs paragraph)? Length? Tone (formal, casual, direct)? What should be avoided?

Let's see this in action:

Before (feels specific, actually vague) "Give me blog post ideas for my SaaS startup."
After (GAC applied) "Give me 5 blog post ideas for a B2B SaaS targeting HR managers at 100-500 person companies. These posts should drive trial sign-ups, not brand awareness. Avoid generic 'industry trends' topics — I want specific pain points HR managers face with employee onboarding. Each idea should include: title, one-sentence premise, and the key question the post answers."

The second prompt takes 30 extra seconds to write. The output is in a completely different league — specific enough to be useful, formatted the way you actually need it.

Why More Context Isn't Always the Answer

Here's the trap many people fall into: they realize they're being too vague, so they compensate by writing longer, more detailed prompts. But without the right structure, more words can make things worse.

One common pattern: users dump everything they know into a prompt ("here's all the context about my business, my product, my users…") and then ask a simple question. The AI gets overwhelmed by the noise and misses the signal.

The solution isn't more context — it's the right context. The GAC framework helps because it forces you to include only the information that shapes the output, rather than dumping everything you know.

As one Reddit user succinctly put it: "I have to push back to get a good answer." The iterative back-and-forth works — but it's exhausting. Closing the specificity gap in your first prompt eliminates most of those iterations.

Real Examples: Before and After the GAC Method

Code Prompt

Before "Write Python code to process data."
After "Write a Python function that reads a CSV with columns [name, email, signup_date] and returns all rows where signup_date is within the last 30 days. Handle missing dates gracefully (skip them, don't error). Use the csv module only — no pandas."

Research Prompt

Before "Tell me about AI in healthcare."
After "Give me a 5-item overview of how AI is currently being used in medical imaging diagnosis (not drug discovery or admin). For each: what it does, what AI model type is typically used, and one real-world example of a hospital or company using it. Keep each item under 100 words."

Writing Prompt

Before "Write product descriptions for our software."
After "Write 3 product descriptions for a project management tool aimed at freelance designers (not teams). Each should be 2 sentences max. Focus on the specific pain (juggling multiple clients, missed deadlines, scope creep) not features. Tone: conversational, like a helpful colleague, not a marketing brochure."

Key Takeaways

  • Specificity lives in the words you type — not in what you meant to say
  • The gap between "clear to you" and "clear to AI" is where generic outputs come from
  • Apply the GAC method: Goal, Audience, Constraints — before every prompt
  • More words aren't better — structured, relevant words are better
  • Tell the AI not just what to do, but what to avoid

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does AI give generic responses even when I feel like my prompt is specific?

Because specificity lives in your head, not in your prompt. AI can't read your intent — it can only act on the words you actually type. If your prompt doesn't explicitly state your goal, audience, and constraints, the AI fills those gaps with generic defaults.

What are the three things most people forget to include in AI prompts?

Most vague prompts are missing: (1) the goal — what success looks like, not just the task; (2) the audience — who is reading this and what do they already know; and (3) constraints — format, length, tone, and what to avoid. Adding these three dramatically improves output quality.

How do I stop AI from making assumptions in my prompts?

State the assumptions explicitly instead of leaving them implicit. Instead of "Write me an email," try "Write me a cold outreach email from a B2B SaaS founder to a VP of Engineering at a 50-200 person company — the recipient has never heard of us and we're asking for a 15-minute call next Tuesday."

What's the difference between a vague prompt and a specific one?

A vague prompt tells the AI what to do. A specific prompt also tells it why, for whom, what success looks like, and what to avoid. Compare "Write an email" (vague) to "Write a follow-up email to a trial user whose 14-day trial ends in 3 days with no usage — the goal is to get them to book a 20-minute onboarding call" (specific).
