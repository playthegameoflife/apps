Telegram is the one major platform where the Bot API cannot schedule for you. The official Telegram Bot API has no schedule_date and no publish_at field — a bot publishes immediately or not at all. So any tool that promises to schedule Telegram posts must own the queue itself and fire the send call at the due moment. The fact that changes everything about Telegram scheduling

Most social platforms let the platform hold your post. Instagram has a publishing scheduler. Mastodon added scheduled_at in version 2.7.0. Reddit's GraphQL API has no scheduling field either, but plenty of third-party tools post to it, so the mechanics are familiar. Telegram is different because its Bot API does not offer a scheduling parameter at all.

Look at the full parameter list for sendMessage in the official documentation: chat_id, text, parse_mode, entities, link_preview_options, disable_notification, protect_content, reply_parameters, reply_markup. Search the page for schedule_date and you get nothing. There is no future-dated send.

What people expect

I hand the post and the time to Telegram, and Telegram keeps it until the moment arrives. Then the scheduler can be shut down and nothing is lost.

What actually happens

The scheduler holds the post. At the due time it calls sendMessage and Telegram publishes immediately. If the scheduler is down at 09:00, the 09:00 post does not exist yet.

That single distinction explains most of the confusion around Telegram scheduling tools. It also means a self-hosted scheduler has to be genuinely always-on, and it means a hosted scheduler with a durable queue is quietly doing something quite valuable.

Three ways to schedule Telegram posts, honestly ranked

1. Telegram's own built-in scheduler (free, one chat at a time)

Telegram has shipped native scheduling in its apps since 2019. Hold the send button in a chat — or right-click it on desktop — choose Schedule Message, and pick a date and time. The post waits in that chat's scheduled list where you can edit it, reschedule it, or send it immediately instead.

There are real limits. You must be an admin with the Post Messages right, and the native scheduler works inside one chat at a time. There is no shared calendar, no bulk spreadsheet import, no way to hold a month of content for twelve channels and release it on a plan. For one channel that you post to by hand, though, it is genuinely good, and it is free.

2. A bot with its own queue (one channel, no calendar)

Several smaller Telegram-first tools work this way. They connect your bot, hold the post, and send it when the clock says so. This solves the “I want the post to leave the channel at 06:00 while I am asleep” problem and nothing more. It is the right answer for a single channel with a simple content rhythm.

3. A unified scheduler with Telegram as one channel among many

How to connect a Telegram channel to Postiz

The setup comes from the official Postiz Telegram documentation, and it is deliberately short. Note that this method is a UI flow — there is no OAuth dance, because a bot token is the credential.

Step 1: create the bot

Message @BotFather, choose Create a new bot, pick a display name and a username ending in bot. BotFather returns an API token. Keep it — this is the credential that lets Postiz post as you.

Step 2: decide on group privacy

BotFather has a Group Privacy setting that is enabled by default. Turn it off if your bot needs to read ordinary group messages. For straight channel posting you can leave it alone, but if you plan to post into discussion groups where replies matter, disabling it avoids surprise silence.

Step 3: add the bot to your channel

Add the bot to the channel or group, then give it the three permissions the docs require: access to messages, Send Text Messages, and Send Media. Promoting it to admin is recommended by the documentation because it grants the full permission set and makes delivery more reliable.

Step 4: hand over the token

.env — self-hosted install TELEGRAM_BOT_NAME="MyPostizBot_bot" TELEGRAM_TOKEN="123456:ABC-DEF1234ghIkl-zyx57W2v1u123ew11"

Add those two values to your environment, restart the containers, and connect the channel in the Postiz interface. Postiz asks you to send /connect followed by a generated word into the channel. The bot is long-polling for that message, checks that it is an admin with delete rights, removes the handshake message, and posts a temporary confirmation that it deletes ten seconds later.

Step 5: understand the one-bot-token rule

This is the gotcha that catches people running Postiz in two places at once. Telegram permits only one connected process per bot token. Postiz uses long-polling getUpdates, so if the same token is used by a cloud instance and a self-hosted instance — or by two self-hosted instances — they will continuously terminate each other and your logs will fill with:

Postiz container log 409 Conflict: terminated by other getUpdates Fix: one TELEGRAM_TOKEN per deployment, one bot per environment.

The documentation states the rule plainly: use one bot token per Postiz deployment, and create a separate bot for each separate environment. The same bot can still post to many channels and groups — the constraint is on the deployment, not on the channel count.

What Postiz does with a Telegram post that other tools get wrong

The provider source in the Postiz repository shows a few behaviours that matter in production, and they are the details you will not find on a marketing page.

Media type is inferred, and files fall back to documents

Postiz inspects the MIME type of each attachment. Images go out as photos and videos as videos. Anything else, including formats Telegram refuses in its photo or video pipelines, is delivered as a document instead — which is why a Postiz Telegram post that would have failed on another tool simply lands. Albums are chunked at ten items, matching Telegram's per-group media ceiling.

A 400 from Telegram is permanent, and Postiz says so

Telegram answers a bad request with HTTP 400 — a caption that is too long, a media file it cannot download. Retrying a 400 can never succeed. Postiz treats that case as a hard failure and surfaces the real reason in the queue instead of silently retrying, including the specific case of a media file Telegram could not fetch from your storage URL. That distinction matters when you self-host: a media URL that is not publicly reachable is a classic cause of a post that never lands.

The text cap is 4,096 characters

That is the provider's maximum length, and it matches Telegram's own limit for a text message. Captions attached to photos and videos are shorter, so a long caption can fail even when the body text would have fit. Keep the body under the cap and treat captions as a separate budget.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you schedule posts on Telegram with a bot?

Not with Telegram's own API. The Bot API sendMessage method has no schedule_date or publish_at parameter, so a bot cannot hand a post to Telegram and walk away. Every third-party scheduler, including Postiz, owns its own queue and fires the send call at the due time, which means the scheduler has to be online when the post is due.

Does Telegram have a built-in message scheduler?

Yes, and it is free. Since 2019 the Telegram apps let you hold the send button, or right-click it on desktop, choose Schedule Message, and pick a date and time. Scheduled posts wait in the chat's scheduled list where you can edit, reschedule, or send them immediately. It requires channel admin rights and works one chat at a time with no shared calendar.

Does Buffer support Telegram scheduling?

No. Buffer, Hootsuite and Later do not list Telegram among their supported channels, which covers Instagram, Facebook, X, LinkedIn, TikTok, YouTube, Pinterest and Threads instead. Dedicated Telegram-first tools such as Nuelink and Publer do support it, and Nuelink adds genuine Telegram-specific strengths like albums of ten items and CSV bulk import. Always confirm against the vendor's current channel list, because these change.

How many Telegram posts can I schedule at once?

Telegram itself does not cap a scheduler's queue, because the scheduler holds the queue rather than Telegram. What does bind you is Telegram's message limits: 4,096 characters per text message, up to 10 media items per album, roughly 20 messages per minute inside one group chat, and about 30 requests per second for the API overall. Shape the calendar so no group gets a burst of queued posts at once.

Do I need a separate bot for each Telegram channel?

One bot can post to many channels and groups, but Telegram allows only one connected process per bot token. Postiz talks to Telegram with long-polling getUpdates, so if the same TELEGRAM_TOKEN is used by two Postiz deployments, each instance kicks the other off and you get 409 Conflict: terminated by other getUpdates in the logs. Use one bot token per deployment, and a separate bot for each separate Postiz environment.

What is the best tool to schedule Telegram posts?

For a single channel you post to by hand, Telegram's native scheduler is free and genuinely enough. For one channel managed alongside X, LinkedIn, Instagram and TikTok, a unified scheduler such as Postiz fits better because one calendar covers every channel. For Telegram-only work with many channels and bulk spreadsheet import, Nuelink or Publer have deeper Telegram-specific features.

Bottom line

To schedule Telegram posts in 2026, the decision is really a question of scope. One channel, posted by hand: use Telegram's free native scheduler and stop there. Many channels, or Telegram alongside your other platforms: you need a tool that owns the queue, because the Bot API will not do it for you, and you need to check that it is always on. Whichever route you take, remember the one rule that causes the most self-hosting pain — one bot token per deployment — and the one limit that shapes your content plan, 4,096 characters per message and ten items per album.
