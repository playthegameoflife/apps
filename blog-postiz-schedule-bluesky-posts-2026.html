Why Bluesky scheduling is different from every other network

Most major networks expose a scheduled_time or publish_at field in their publishing API. You hand the network a timestamp and the network does the work. Bluesky does not have one. Bluesky runs on the AT Protocol, and a post is a repository record of type app.bsky.feed.post with a text field and a createdAt timestamp. There is no field that says "not yet, publish later."

That means a Bluesky scheduler is, mechanically, a small server with a timer attached. It stores your draft, sleeps, and at the due moment calls com.atproto.repo.createRecord on your personal data server. There is no trick to route around it and no native fallback if your scheduler is down at 9:00am.

The native-scheduler myth

Mastodon has scheduled single posts built into its own web and mobile clients for years. People assume "the fediverse" works the same way. It does not. Bluesky's app still has no scheduler at all, so a tool that promises to post on Bluesky is promising to do the publishing itself.

What "schedule Bluesky posts" actually requires

Before choosing a tool, know the four constraints you are signing up for. Each one kills a naive setup.

1. An app password, not your account password

Bluesky will not accept your login password for third-party publishing. You generate an app password in Settings → App Passwords, with at least atproto.create scope. The tool trades that token for a session and publishes as you. Postiz asks for the handle and app password straight from the channel setup screen — no environment variables, no callback URLs to register. You can also revoke an individual app password later without touching your account password or other sessions.

2. A 300 grapheme limit, not 300 characters

Graphemes are user-perceived characters, so an emoji or an accented script eats more budget than it looks. Long posts are the single most common reason a scheduled Bluesky post fails validation at the composer.

3. Media rules that differ per network

Images upload as blobs through com.atproto.repo.uploadBlob with a 2 MB limit, and up to four can ride along on one post. Video travels the same record path. The same asset does not have identical rules on Mastodon, so a cross-post needs per-channel handling rather than a single global payload.

4. Per-post visibility and content warnings

Mastodon exposes a visibility level (public, unlisted, followers-only, direct) and content-warning fields per post. Bluesky does not have those concepts. If you run both, one shared payload will not cover them — you need a composer that stores a per-channel variant.

How the same announcement is queued for two networks Bluesky — 300 grapheme limit, no visibility field:
{"text": "Shipped the 2.4 release today. Full changelog on the blog — link in replies.", "createdAt": "2026-10-23T14:00:00Z"}

Mastodon — 500 char default, visibility + CW supported:
{"status": "Shipped the 2.4 release today. Full changelog on the blog: https://example.com/changelog", "visibility": "unlisted", "scheduled_at": "2026-10-23T14:00:00Z"}

Note the asymmetry in the last line. Mastodon accepts scheduled_at and the network publishes it. Bluesky has no equivalent field, so the same scheduler that hands Mastodon a future timestamp has to hold the Bluesky post in its own queue and call createRecord at 14:00. Understanding that difference is the difference between a tool that works and a tool that silently posts three hours late.

Three ways to schedule Bluesky posts, honestly compared

Option 1 — Bluesky-native and Mastodon-native tools

These are the fastest for a single network. A Bluesky-only scheduler can offer genuinely better per-platform timing suggestions than a generalist, and Mastodon's own client handles scheduled posts with no third party involved at all. The catch is obvious: you are running two separate calendars, two approval flows, and two sets of analytics. If your audience overlaps between a Mastodon instance and Bluesky, that is a real cost in daily effort, not a rounding error.

Option 2 — Unified schedulers

Buffer, Hootsuite, Sprout Social, and Postiz all reach Bluesky, and each treats it as one channel among many on a shared calendar. The strength is operational: one content calendar, one team approval flow, one analytics view. The weakness is that the Bluesky integration gets the same generic treatment as the others — you will still write a short Bluesky variant by hand unless the tool supports per-channel copy, which is exactly why per-channel editing is worth checking for before you pay.

Option 3 — DIY with cron and the AT Protocol SDK

If you already run infrastructure, this is viable and free. The AT Protocol SDK makes creating a post a short script. The hard parts are not the API call — they are session refresh, media upload, retries when the PDS is briefly unavailable, and the queue surviving a restart. The honest cost is maintenance: someone has to own the timer, and when it breaks the post simply does not go out.

Key Takeaways

  • Bluesky has no native scheduler and no scheduled_time field — every tool that posts there is publishing on your behalf at the due minute.
  • Mastodon does schedule natively, which is why the two networks behave differently even though both use the AT Protocol.
  • Connection requires an app password with atproto.create scope, generated in Bluesky Settings, not your account password.
  • The real limit is 300 graphemes, with 2 MB image blobs and a four-image maximum per post.
  • Per-channel copy is not a nicety — the same payload cannot satisfy a 300-grapheme Bluesky post and a Mastodon post with a visibility setting.

Choosing a Bluesky scheduler: what to check before you pay

  • Per-channel copy editing. If the tool only has one global text field, you will be rewriting every cross-post by hand.
