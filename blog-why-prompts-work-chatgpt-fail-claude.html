The Real Reason Your Prompts Fail Across AI Models

Here's what Reddit communities have been screaming about for two years: "What works for ChatGPT doesn't work for Claude." The thread gets thousands of upvotes every time it surfaces, because it's a universal pain point with no obvious fix.

The root cause isn't user error. Each major AI model has its own "language preference":

  • ChatGPT — responds to conversational clarity, markdown formatting, and explicit "do this, not that" instructions. It handles longer prompts well and benefits from role-framing ("act as a senior software engineer...").
  • Claude — prefers structured prompts with XML tags, explicit role definitions, and constraint statements. Plain conversational prompts often get interpreted loosely; Claude shines when instructions are boxed in XML delimiters.
  • Gemini — tends to deprioritize instructions in long paragraphs and works better with numbered, short-instruction formats. Gemini also benefits from explicit "think step by step" chains for reasoning tasks.
  • Grok — optimized for direct, unfiltered responses; responds well to assertive framing and less hedging language.
  • Perplexity — designed for research and citation; works better with source-seeking and comparison-framed prompts than creative or open-ended ones.

The same 50-word prompt, copied verbatim across all five, will produce five noticeably different results — sometimes dramatically so. This isn't a bug. It's the inevitable result of different training data, architectures, and optimization targets.

How to Write Prompts That Work Across All AI Models

There are two paths: manually adapt your prompting style per model, or use a tool that does it automatically. Both approaches are valid; the manual approach gives you more control, the tool approach saves enormous time. Here's the manual framework first, so you understand the underlying structure.

The Universal Prompt Structure: Task + Context + Constraints

Every prompt that works across models has three components, made explicit:

  • Task — What exactly you want the AI to do. Be a verb: "Write," "Analyze," "Compare," "Summarize."
  • Context — Who it's for, why it matters, what situation it's in. The AI uses this to calibrate tone and depth.
  • Constraints — What format, length, tone, audience, and what to avoid. These are the most commonly omitted — and the most impactful.

Here's the difference between a weak prompt and a well-constrained one:

Weak prompt (generic output risk) Write a cold email for our SaaS product.
Well-constrained prompt (platform-agnostic structure) Write a cold email for a B2B SaaS company selling project management software. Task: Write one cold email (150-180 words). Context: The recipient is a operations manager at a 50-200 person company. They are busy and skeptical of vendor claims. Constraints: - Tone: Friendly but direct, no buzzwords - Hook: Reference a specific pain point (missed deadlines or scope creep), not generic "boost productivity" language - Structure: Hook line → 2 sentence context → brief value prop (1 sentence) → CTA to book a 15-min demo - Avoid: Paragraphs over 3 sentences, passive voice, any mention of "AI-powered" or "cutting-edge"

The second version works better everywhere — because the AI doesn't have to guess what you want. But even this improved version still benefits from platform-specific formatting (XML tags for Claude, numbered format for Gemini, markdown for ChatGPT).

The Model-Specific Tweaks That Matter Most

Once you have the universal structure locked in, here are the adjustments that move the needle per model:

  • For Claude: Wrap your prompt sections in XML tags (<task>, <context>, <constraints>, <output>). Claude is specifically trained to weight content within XML delimiters more heavily. Without tags, it infers structure from plain text — and sometimes infers wrong.
  • For Gemini: Use numbered short-instruction lists instead of paragraphs. Gemini's context window handling deprioritizes content in long prose blocks. Numbered constraints get read first and followed more reliably.
  • For ChatGPT: Use markdown formatting (headers, bullet points, bold). ChatGPT is optimized for markdown-readable input and produces better structured output when the prompt uses it.
  • For Grok: Use assertive framing and minimize hedging language ("might," "could," "perhaps"). Grok is trained to be direct and responds to confidence-oriented instruction styles.
  • For Perplexity: Frame the task as a research question or comparison rather than open-ended generation. Perplexity's strengths are in sourcing and synthesis — prompting it as a search engine rather than a writer yields better results.

Common Questions About Prompting Across AI Models

Why does the same prompt give different results on ChatGPT vs Claude?

Each AI model was trained on different data, uses different architectures, and optimizes for different response patterns. A prompt written for ChatGPT's style can read as vague, repetitive, or off-target to Claude, which prefers structured XML tags and explicit constraints over conversational framing.

Do I need to write different prompts for each AI model?

What prompting style works best for ChatGPT?

ChatGPT responds best to clear, direct instructions with explicit output format requests. Use markdown formatting, specify the tone and audience, and state exactly what you want avoided. Concise but complete beats vague but lengthy.

What prompting style works best for Claude?

Claude prefers XML-tagged prompts with clear role definitions and explicit constraints. Wrapping context in <input> tags, defining the task in <task> tags, and specifying output format in <output> tags significantly improves Claude's response quality compared to plain conversational prompts.

Why does my prompt get ignored when I switch to Gemini?

Gemini often truncates very long prompts and deprioritizes instructions buried in long paragraphs. Gemini responds better to shorter, numbered instructions and explicit step-by-step constraints. The fix: break long prompts into numbered points and lead with the most important instruction.

Can one prompt work across all AI models?

A base prompt can work everywhere if it covers three things: the task (what to do), the context (why it matters and who it's for), and the constraints (how to format the output and what to avoid).
