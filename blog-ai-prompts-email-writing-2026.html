Cold Outreach Emails (Prompts 1–5)

Cold emails fail when they're vague. The AI doesn't know your prospect's name, their company, or why they should care — until you tell it. These prompts are built to force specificity.

Cold Outreach — Initial Contact

Prompt #1: The Personalized Cold Email

Use this when reaching out to someone who has never heard of you. It structures the email around them, not you.

Write a cold outreach email with these exact specifications:

RECIPIENT: [their name], [their job title] at [their company]
RECENT CONTEXT: [one specific thing they recently did — a product launch, a blog post, a job change, an award — be specific]
MY VALUE: [one concrete outcome I can deliver, not a feature list]
MY BACKGROUND: [one sentence establishing credibility — a client I've helped, a result I've achieved]
ASK: [specific, low-commitment ask — 15-min call, reply to a question, etc.]

TONE: [conversational / direct / warm / professional]
LENGTH: Under 150 words
STRUCTURE: Hook line referencing their context → one sentence on me → 2-3 sentences on the outcome I deliver → specific ask → short sign-off
CONSTRAINT: Do NOT use the phrases "I hope this email finds you well," "I wanted to reach out because," or "I'd love to connect."
Pro tip: If you don't know anything specific about your recipient, replace the "Recent Context" line with industry pain point research. Something like "Their company is in a market that's shifting toward [trend]" still outperforms a fully generic email.
Cold Outreach — Warm Introduction

Prompt #2: The Mutual Connection Email

When you have a referral or mutual connection, this prompt positions that relationship as the natural opener — without being stiff about it.

Write a warm introduction email using this structure:

WHO REFERRED ME: [referrer's name and relationship to recipient — colleague, former client, industry peer, etc.]
WHAT THEY SAID: [one specific thing the referrer said about the recipient or their company]
MY CONTEXT: [one sentence — what I do and who I typically help]
THE RECIPIENT'S SITUATION: [describe their current challenge or goal in 1-2 sentences, based on what you know about them]
THE OUTCOME: [what I believe I can help them achieve — be specific]
MY ASK: [low-commitment ask that acknowledges the referrer's trust]

TONE: Warm but professional — like a friend making a thoughtful intro, not a salesperson
LENGTH: Under 180 words
CONSTRAINT: Start with the referrer's name, not "I was speaking with [name]." Name them first.
Cold Outreach — Guest Post / Collab Request

Prompt #3: The Collaboration Pitch

Use this when pitching guest posts, podcast appearances, partnerships, or cross-promotion. Editors and partners respond to emails that demonstrate you know their work.

Write a collaboration pitch email with these elements:

THEIR WORK: [specific piece of their content (article, podcast, product) you've engage with — include a concrete detail]
MY UNIQUE ANGLE: [what I bring that specifically complements their work — not generic credentials]
THE SPECIFIC PROPOSAL: [exactly what you're proposing — guest post on [topic], podcast episode about [theme], etc.]
MUTUAL BENEFIT: [one sentence on why this helps their audience or their goals]
SOCIAL PROOF (if relevant): [your own audience size, past placements, or results relevant to their niche]

TONE: Confident but respectful — you know your stuff, and you're proposing a partnership, not asking for a favor
LENGTH: Under 200 words
STRUCTURE: Hook referencing their work → your angle → specific proposal → mutual benefit → soft ask to continue the conversation
Cold Outreach — LinkedIn Profile View

Prompt #4: The LinkedIn Connection Note

Use this as your connection request note on LinkedIn. It's the 300-character impression before you can message them directly.

Write a LinkedIn connection request note (under 300 characters) with these requirements:

RECIPIENT: [their name], [their job title]
WHY I'M REACHING OUT: [one specific reason — mutual interest, saw their work on [topic], etc.]
WHAT I OFFER OR WANT: [one clear value statement or request]
TONE: Direct and specific — LinkedIn rewards brevity; no fluff

CONSTRAINT: This must feel like a real human who did 5 minutes of research, not a template. Avoid: "I'd love to connect and pick your brain."
Pro tip: LinkedIn now allows 1500 characters in connection notes. If you're serious about LinkedIn outreach, ask AI for a "short version" (under 300 chars) for the connection note and a "long version" (under 1500 chars) to send after they accept as a follow-up message.
Cold Outreach — Investor / Partnership

Prompt #5: The Formal Partnership Inquiry

Use this for reaching out to potential investors, resellers, enterprise partners, or licensing contacts. It reads formal without being stiff.

Write a formal partnership inquiry email with these specifics:

MY COMPANY: [company name] — [one sentence describing what you do and who you serve]
PARTNERSHIP TYPE: [describe exactly what kind of partnership you're proposing — co-marketing, referral, distribution, investment, etc.]
WHY THEM SPECIFICALLY: [one specific reason this partner makes sense — their audience, their product fit, their market position]
CURRENT SCALE: [metrics that establish credibility — users, revenue, audience size, growth rate]
WHAT I'M ASKING FOR: [specific ask — intro call, proposal review, term sheet discussion]
NEXT STEP: [suggest the first concrete next step]

TONE: Professional, concise, confident. Pitch deck energy in prose.
LENGTH: Under 250 words
STRUCTURE: Company intro → partnership type → why them → credibility signal → specific ask → proposed next step

Follow-Up Emails (Prompts 6–9)

Follow-ups are where most people give up too early — and where AI can help you be persistent without feeling pushy. The key variable: tell AI exactly how many times you've already followed up and whether you want to escalate or close out.

Follow-Up — Standard Nudge

Prompt #6: The Non-Awkward Follow-Up

Use this 3-5 days after your initial email with no response. It adds value without repeating yourself.

Write a follow-up email with these parameters:

ORIGINAL EMAIL DATE: [date you sent the first email]
ORIGINAL SUBJECT: [the subject line you used]
RECIPIENT'S ROLE: [their job title, which tells AI how busy they probably are]
WHAT I OFFERED: [one sentence summarizing your original value proposition]
NEW VALUE I'M ADDING: [one new piece of information — a relevant stat, a case study snippet, an upcoming development, a question reframed]
HOW MANY PREVIOUS FOLLOW-UPS: [0 if this is the first follow-up, 1 if it's the second, etc.]

TONE: Friendly, helpful, not pushy. Acknowledge they may have been busy.
LENGTH: Under 120 words
CONSTRAINT: Do NOT say "just following up" or "just wanted to check in." Lead with the new value.
Follow-Up — After a Meeting

Prompt #7: The Post-Meeting Recap Email

Send this within 24 hours of any sales call, discovery call, or interview. It positions you as organized and reliable.

Write a post-meeting recap email with these details:

MEETING TOPIC: [what you discussed]
DATE AND DURATION: [when it happened, how long]
KEY DECISIONS MADE: [bullet-list every decision or agreement made in the meeting]
ACTION ITEMS: [bullet-list what each person agreed to do — include deadlines if mentioned]
OPEN QUESTIONS: [anything that was left unresolved or needs further discussion]
NEXT STEPS: [the confirmed next meeting or call if one was scheduled]

TONE: Clear, professional, action-oriented. This is a working document as much as an email.
LENGTH: As long as it takes to be useful — clarity over brevity here.
FORMAT: Use bullet points. Label each section clearly.
Follow-Up — Second or Third Nudge

Prompt #8: The Persistent-but-Polite Follow-Up

Use this for the 2nd or 3rd follow-up when you haven't heard back. The tone shifts from "just checking in" to acknowledging the silence and offering a clear exit.

Write a follow-up email for someone who hasn't responded to [number: 2/3/4] previous emails.

CONTEXT: I sent my first email on [date] about [one sentence on what you offered]. I've followed up on [dates of follow-ups] without a response.
RECIPIENT'S SITUATION: [their likely context — they're busy, they may have missed it, they may not be interested]
MY OPTIONS NOW: [choose one: close out gracefully / offer a different format / send a final "closing the loop" email / escalate in some way]

TONE: Confident without being pushy. You're offering a clear off-ramp: "If this isn't relevant, just say so."
LENGTH: Under 100 words
CONSTRAINT: Include a subject line refresh suggestion in brackets — a new subject line can often get a stale thread reopened.
Follow-Up — Trade Show / Conference

Prompt #9: The Conference Follow-Up

Follow up after meeting someone at a conference, meetup, or event. The window is short — do this within 48 hours while you're still fresh in their memory.

Write a conference follow-up email:

EVENT: [name of the event]
WHERE WE MET: [specific context — "you were demoing at booth 47," "we spoke after the keynote on AI tools," etc.]
WHAT WE DISCUSSED: [one sentence on the specific topic or challenge they mentioned]
MY SPECIFIC OFFER: [relate your product or service directly to the challenge they described]
PERSONAL HOOK: [one specific thing that made your conversation memorable — a shared interest, a joke, a connection you made]
ATTACHMENT IDEA: [suggest whether to attach something — your deck, a relevant article, your one-pager, or nothing]

TONE: Warm and personal — you met them at a live event, so this should feel human
LENGTH: Under 150 words
SUBJECT LINE: Suggest 3 subject lines — one using the event name, one personal, one benefit-oriented

Customer Response Emails (Prompts 10–13)

Customer emails require the most care — the recipient is already engaged with your product or company, and the stakes for tone are higher. AI works well here when you give it the full context of the customer's message and what outcome you want.

Customer Response — Handling a Complaint

Prompt #10: The Complaint Response

Use this when a customer is upset, frustrated, or disappointed. The goal is to acknowledge without over-apologizing and move toward resolution.

Write a customer complaint response email:

CUSTOMER'S ISSUE: [describe what happened from the customer's perspective — use their words if you have them]
CUSTOMER'S EMOTIONAL STATE (if known): [frustrated / confused / disappointed / angry]
WHAT WENT WRONG: [one honest sentence on what caused the issue — do not deflect]
WHAT I'M DOING TO FIX IT: [specific action you're taking — refund, replacement, credit, fix in progress]
WHAT I'M DOING TO PREVENT RECURRENCE: [one concrete step to stop this from happening again]
COMPENSATION (if any): [state clearly what the customer gets — if nothing, be graceful about it]

TONE: Genuine, not groveling. Customers respect honest accountability more than elaborate apologies.
LENGTH: Under 180 words
STRUCTURE: Acknowledgment of their frustration (one sentence, specific, not "I'm sorry for the inconvenience") → what went wrong → what you're doing now → what you're doing to prevent it → clear next step
Customer Response — Feature Request Acknowledgment

Prompt #11: The Feature Request Response

Use this when a customer asks for a feature your product doesn't have yet. It validates their request without overpromising.

Write a response to a customer who requested a feature:

FEATURE THEY ASKED FOR: [the specific feature or capability they described]
WHY IT'S VALUABLE: [one sentence on the use case they mentioned — shows you listened]
OUR CURRENT STATUS: [honest status — planned for Q[X], under review, on the roadmap, or not currently planned]
WHAT THEY CAN DO IN THE MEANTIME: [specific workaround, alternative approach, or setting that partially addresses their need]
WHEN THEY'LL HEAR MORE: [realistic timeline for follow-up, or a link to your public roadmap]

TONE: Appreciative and honest. The customer took time to write in — validate that.
LENGTH: Under 140 words
CONSTRAINT: Do NOT say "great suggestion!" repeatedly. One genuine acknowledgment is enough.
Customer Response — Onboarding Check-In

Prompt #12: The Onboarding Check-In

Use this at day 3, day 7, or day 14 of a new customer's onboarding to proactively address friction before they churn.

Write a proactive onboarding check-in email:

CUSTOMER'S GOAL: [what they're trying to achieve with your product — be specific, based on their signup context or sales conversation]
EXPECTED DAY: [which day of onboarding this is — Day 3, Day 7, etc.]
COMMON STUCK POINT THIS DAY: [name the specific thing most customers struggle with at this stage — gives the email a useful hook]
SPECIFIC HELP I'M OFFERING: [one concrete thing you can do right now — a setup step, a call to book, a guide to read]
NEXT NATURAL MILESTONE: [what they should be able to do after completing this step]

TONE: Warm, helpful, not pushy. You're offering a hand, not asking for a meeting.
LENGTH: Under 130 words
INCLUDE: A single clear CTA — one link, one action.
Customer Response — Win-Back / Re-engagement

Prompt #13: The Re-engagement Email

Use this for customers who signed up, used the product, then went quiet. It re-engages without guilt-tripping.

Write a re-engagement email for an inactive customer:

WHAT THEY SIGNED UP FOR: [the core value your product delivers]
WHAT THEY LAST DID: [last login date, last feature they used, or last email you sent them — be specific]
WHAT'S CHANGED: [new features launched, new use cases discovered, improvements made since they left]
SPECIFIC RE-ENTRY OFFER: [a concrete, low-friction offer to come back — "try X again," "unlock Y for free," "jump into Z workflow"]
WHY NOW: [a specific reason to come back — time of year, a feature they've been waiting for, a new use case]

TONE: Welcoming, not guilt-inducing. No "we miss you" — show them what they're missing.
LENGTH: Under 150 words
SUBJECT LINE: Offer 3 options — one curiosity-driven, one benefit-driven, one direct.

Internal & Workplace Emails (Prompts 14–16)

Internal emails are often where AI email writing shines brightest — the stakes are lower and the volume is higher. But the same specificity rules apply. Don't tell AI "write an email to the team." Tell it who, what, when, and why.

Internal — Meeting Request

Prompt #14: The Effective Meeting Request

Use this instead of the vague "Can we jump on a call?" email. It gives the recipient context to decide — and prepares them to show up ready.

Write a meeting request email:

TOPIC: [what you want to discuss — be specific, not "quick question"]
WHY IT'S URGENT (if applicable): [deadline, blocker, decision needed by, external dependency]
WHY THEY NEED TO BE THERE: [what perspective or decision-making authority they bring]
WHAT I'VE ALREADY TRIED: [steps you've already taken to answer this yourself — shows respect for their time]
MEETING FORMAT: [in-person / video call / phone]
PROPOSED TIMES: [2-3 specific time slots with timezones]
PROPOSED DURATION: [15 / 30 / 45 / 60 minutes]
PRE-WORK (if any): [anything they should read or prepare beforehand]

TONE: Respectful of their time. Make it easy to say yes.
LENGTH: Under 150 words
FORMAT: Subject line should be the topic, not "Meeting Request."
Internal — Project Update to Manager

Prompt #15: The Status Update Email

Use this weekly or at project milestones. It gives your manager the signal without requiring a meeting.

Write a project status update email for [manager name / team]:

PROJECT: [project name]
OVERALL STATUS: [on track / at risk / blocked / complete]
THIS WEEK'S PROGRESS: [2-3 bullet points on what was accomplished]
DECISIONS OR ASSUMPTIONS MADE: [anything you decided without approval that they should know about]
BLOCKERS OR RISKS: [specific blockers with owners — who's responsible for unblocking each one]
NEXT WEEK'S PLAN: [2-3 bullet points on what's coming]
DECISIONS NEEDED FROM THEM: [0-3 specific questions requiring their input]

TONE: Concise, factual, action-oriented. Your manager is skimming this.
LENGTH: As long as needed, but front-load the status line.
FORMAT: Label each section. Use bold for status, blockers, and decisions needed.
Internal — Delivering Difficult Feedback

Prompt #16: The Constructive Feedback Email

Use this when you need to deliver feedback to a colleague, direct report, or cross-functional partner — in writing, when a meeting isn't practical or you want to give them time to process.

Write a constructive feedback email:

WHO YOU'RE FEEDBACKING: [their name, role]
SITUATION: [what happened — describe specific observable behavior, not personality]
IMPACT: [the effect of their behavior on you, the team, or the project]
YOUR INTERPRETATION: [what you think was behind it — "I suspect you were under deadline pressure" — gives them the benefit of the doubt]
WHAT YOU'D LIKE TO SEE DIFFERENTLY: [specific alternative behavior, stated positively]
YOUR SUPPORT: [what you can do to help — resources, coaching, clarification, etc.]
NEXT STEP: [propose a follow-up conversation or check-in to revisit this]

TONE: Direct but compassionate. You're delivering feedback as a peer or collaborator, not as a judge.
LENGTH: Under 250 words
CONSTRAINT: Do not use "I feel" statements when describing impact — use "the effect was" or "the result was." Keep emotions out of the description and in the framing only.

Sales & Closing Emails (Prompts 17–19)

Sales emails have the highest stakes — one wrong word and the deal cools. These prompts are built to be direct without being pushy, specific without being creepy, and confident without being arrogant.

Sales — Proposal Follow-Up

Prompt #17: The Proposal Follow-Up

Use this 5-7 days after sending a proposal with no response. It reframes the conversation without repeating the pitch.

Write a proposal follow-up email:

PROPOSAL SENT: [date you sent the proposal]
PROPOSAL SUMMARY: [one sentence on what you proposed — scope, solution, price range if appropriate]
WHAT CHANGED SINCE: [anything new — market development, a competitor development, a relevant stat published, a new milestone you hit]
REFRAMED QUESTION: [reframe the core question from a new angle — instead of "any questions?" ask something specific]
SOFT OFF-RAMP: [give them an easy way to say no or delay — "if timing isn't right, just say so" — this paradoxically increases response rates]

TONE: Confident, not pushy. You're not chasing — you're opening a door.
LENGTH: Under 130 words
CONSTRAINT: Do NOT attach the proposal again unless they specifically ask. It just creates more for them to open.
Sales — Closing Email

Prompt #18: The Closing Email

Use this when you're ready to close — sending a contract, asking for a signature, or requesting final approval to move forward.

Write a closing email with these details:

WHAT YOU'RE ASKING FOR: [contract signature / proposal approval / final budget sign-off / etc.]
WHAT'S IN IT FOR THEM: [recap the outcome they're getting — be specific]
WHAT HAPPENS NEXT: [the immediate next steps after they sign / approve — how fast does implementation start?]
WHAT IF THEY HESITATE: [if there's a specific objection they've raised, address it preemptively in one sentence]
THANK YOU: [genuine thanks for their time in the process — specific to a moment in your conversations]

TONE: Warm but business-confident. You're closing a deal, not asking permission.
LENGTH: Under 120 words
CTA: One clear button or link — everything else should serve that action.
Sales — Referral Request

Prompt #19: The Referral Request

Use this after a successful sale or positive interaction — when the customer is happy and you want to leverage that moment into warm leads.

Write a referral request email:

THEIR OUTCOME: [specific result they achieved with your product or service — use their words if possible]
WHY REFERRALS MATTER NOW: [one sentence on why you're asking now specifically — new hire, new product, new market, end of quarter]
THE TYPE OF REFERRAL: [specific — "one company that's facing the same challenge you were" / "a peer who mentioned this problem to you at [event]" / etc.]
WHAT YOU'LL DO WITH THE INTRO: [one sentence on how you'll handle the warm intro to protect their reputation]
HOW TO DO IT: [simple mechanism — "just reply with their name and I'll take it from there" / "use this referral link" / etc.]

TONE: Grateful and specific. You're asking for something valuable, so make it easy and low-risk.
LENGTH: Under 130 words
CONSTRAINT: Do NOT offer cash incentives in the same email as the ask — it cheapens the request. Handle referral bonuses in a separate email.

Quick-Reply Templates (Prompt 20)

Sometimes you need a full email written from scratch in under two minutes. This is your emergency prompt.

Universal Quick Draft

Prompt #20: The Anything-You-Need Email

Use this for any situation not covered above — give it the full context and get a usable first draft.

Write a professional email for the following situation:

RECIPIENT: [their name, title, and relationship to you — client / colleague / prospect / partner / friend-of-a-friend]
SITUATION: [describe the full context in 2-4 sentences — what happened, what's the current state, what triggered this email]
PURPOSE OF THE EMAIL: [inform / persuade / update / request / respond / apologize / connect]
DESIRED OUTCOME: [what you want to happen as a result of this email — a meeting, a decision, a reply, their understanding]
TONE: [conversational / formal / warm / direct / empathetic / confident]
TONE EXAMPLES (paste 1-2 sentences of your own writing style here, or describe it — "short sentences, no jargon, direct"):

LENGTH: [Under X words — be specific]
MUST INCLUDE: [anything specific the email must contain — a deadline, a CTA, a reference number, etc.]
MUST AVOID: [phrases, tones, or approaches that would feel wrong in this context]

CONSTRAINT: The first line of the email should do work — either hook the reader or acknowledge the relationship. Do not open with "I hope this finds you well" or "I wanted to reach out."

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best AI prompt for cold emails?

The best AI prompt for cold emails includes your recipient's name, their company or recent achievement, your value proposition in one sentence, and a specific ask. Templates #1 and #2 in this post give you ready-to-use versions of exactly this structure.

Can AI write professional emails that sound human?

Yes — AI writes professional emails that sound human when you give it your voice cues: your industry jargon, your sentence length preference, and examples of emails you've sent. The prompt template in this article includes all three of these elements.

How do I use AI for email without it sounding generic?

To avoid generic AI emails: add specific details (a real pain point, a recent event about their company, your unique angle), specify your tone in the prompt (conversational, direct, empathetic), and always edit the output to add one personal touch before sending.

What AI model is best for email writing?

Claude and GPT-4o are the strongest for email writing in 2026. Claude tends to produce more natural, flowing prose; GPT-4o responds well to structured instructions. Gemini works for simple emails but may need more tone guidance for formal business correspondence.

How do I write a follow-up email with AI?

Use prompt #6 and #8 in this article: include the original email subject line, the date you sent it, the recipient's role, and what outcome you're following up on. Specify whether you want a gentle nudge or a more direct closing line.
